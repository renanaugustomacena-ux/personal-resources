## Introduction — Why Most Backtests Are Wrong

A backtest is a simulated history. It looks like a P&L curve, has a Sharpe ratio, computes a maximum drawdown — all the trappings of a real track record. But the simulation is generated by *applying a strategy chosen with knowledge of the data* to that same data. The probability that this exercise generates a positive result purely by chance is much higher than the simulation's confidence intervals suggest.

The published academic and industry literature is dominated by spurious findings. Harvey and Liu (2014) document 316 "anomaly" factors in finance literature, the vast majority of which fail to replicate out-of-sample. Bailey and López de Prado have argued that fewer than 1% of "back-tested" strategies in industry would survive proper statistical scrutiny.

The practical question is: how do we build backtests we can actually trust? Three principles:

1. **Account for everything you can measure**: transaction costs, borrow rates, capacity, survivorship-corrected universes, point-in-time data.
2. **Account for the multiple-testing problem**: you tried many strategies before this one; control for the resulting false-positive rate.
3. **Validate out-of-sample**: not just on a held-out period, but on multiple held-out periods, regime variations, and live paper-trading.

This document develops the statistical machinery and the operational discipline. We assume basic familiarity with hypothesis testing and the standard concepts of mean and variance. The mathematical content is pragmatic, not exhaustive — we focus on the tools that change real decisions.

A note on philosophy. Frequentist statistics asks: "If the strategy had no edge, how often would I see a Sharpe this high by chance?" Bayesian statistics asks: "Given my prior beliefs and the evidence, what is the probability the strategy has edge?" Both are valid; both are used. The frequentist tools dominate this document because they are easier to apply universally, but Bayesian methods (document 203) are equally valuable when prior structure is available.

## Survivorship Bias

The most common bias. The dataset includes only firms (or assets) that *survived* to today. Firms that went bankrupt, were delisted, or merged out of existence are excluded.

### Why It Matters

Survivorship-biased backtests systematically overestimate returns:
- Firms that disappeared often did so after losing most of their value.
- A "long the equity market" strategy run on a survivorship-biased universe earns 1-3% per year more than the true market return.
- Mutual fund return series exclude funds that closed (often the worst-performing).

### Example

If you backtest a "buy SPX stocks" strategy using the *current* SPX 500 constituent list and historical prices, you've already preselected for winners. Companies that were in SPX in 1990 but went bankrupt or were delisted are excluded. The backtest looks great; live trading would be different.

### Fix: Point-in-Time (PIT) Universes

Use a universe constructed *as of each historical date*. Common sources:
- **CRSP**: standard academic source for US equities; includes delisting returns.
- **Compustat Point-in-Time**: includes only data available at each historical date.
- **Bloomberg PIT**: index constituents as of each date.
- **Custom**: build your own PIT universe from primary sources.

### Reality Check — Vendor Data Sneakiness

Many commercial data vendors deliver "current universe" by default. Survivorship-corrected requires explicit request. Verify by checking that delisted tickers exist in the historical data.

## Look-Ahead Bias

Using future information in a backtest decision. Subtle and varied:

### Common Sources

- **Centered moving average**: average over [t-5, t+5] uses future. Use trailing only.
- **Closing price for trading decision**: if decision is at 4:00pm using 4:00pm price, you cannot trade at that price. Use 3:55pm or model the auction.
- **Earnings data on report day**: earnings reported at 4:30pm, but the report is dated for the close. If your data is point-in-time, the date is correct; if not, you use earnings before they are public.
- **Restated financials**: if a company restates earnings 6 months later, the historical record now contains the restated number. The original number was different.
- **Index reconstitution**: if a stock joins SPX next month, you don't know that today.

### Detection

Compare backtest performance using:
- **Strict PIT data** (only what was available).
- **As-reported data** (what's in the database today).

Differences indicate look-ahead.

### Reality Check — Look-Ahead Anywhere

Look-ahead can creep in via:
- Joining datasets with timestamps that don't match.
- Computing features on z-scores using full-sample mean/std.
- Running forecasts using fitted parameters from the entire sample.

Audit every step of the data pipeline.

## Selection Bias and the Factor Zoo

When researchers test many factors and report only the ones that work, the reported results are biased upward.

### The Factor Zoo

Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016): cataloged 316 factors in academic literature. Most have t-stats below the 3+ threshold typically claimed for credibility. Cochrane (2011) called this the "factor zoo."

### Hidden Multiple Testing

A factor with t = 2.5 in published research:
- If the researcher tested 1 factor: highly significant.
- If the researcher tested 100 factors: probably noise (5% by chance).

Without knowing how many factors were tested, you cannot assess true significance.

### Multiple Testing Correction

For k tests with desired family-wise error rate α:

**Bonferroni**: use α/k per test. Conservative. For 100 tests at α = 0.05: per-test α = 0.0005, requires t ~ 3.5.

**Holm**: stepwise version, less conservative. Sort p-values; compare i-th to α/(k − i + 1).

**Šidák**: 1 − (1 − α)^{1/k}. Slightly less conservative than Bonferroni.

**Benjamini–Hochberg FDR**: controls expected false discovery rate. Sort p-values; reject if p_(i) < (i/k)α.

**Storey's q-value**: estimates proportion of true nulls and computes adjusted p-values.

### Practical Rule

For a single-strategy claim with no formal correction, t > 3 is the credibility threshold. For one of K candidates, t > 3.5 + 0.5 log_10(K) is the rough rule.

```python
from scipy.stats import norm

def required_t_for_significance(num_tested, alpha=0.05):
    """Bonferroni-corrected t-stat for given number of tests."""
    p_corrected = alpha / num_tested
    return norm.ppf(1 - p_corrected/2)

print(f"For 1 test: t > {required_t_for_significance(1):.2f}")
print(f"For 10 tests: t > {required_t_for_significance(10):.2f}")
print(f"For 100 tests: t > {required_t_for_significance(100):.2f}")
print(f"For 10000 tests: t > {required_t_for_significance(10000):.2f}")
```

## Multiple Testing

We covered the corrections above. The deeper challenge in trading: you don't always know how many tests were "really" done.

### Implicit Multiple Testing

- Trying multiple lookback windows (5d, 10d, 20d, 50d, 100d).
- Trying multiple thresholds (1%, 2%, 5%).
- Trying multiple universes (SPX, R2K, sectors, individual stocks).
- Trying multiple regression specifications.

A "single backtest" can hide hundreds of implicit tests.

### Discipline

- Pre-register strategies before testing.
- Document every variant tried.
- Use information criteria (AIC, BIC) for model selection.
- Adjust significance thresholds for honest test count.

## Sharpe Ratio Inference

The reported Sharpe ratio is a sample estimate; the population Sharpe is unknown. Sharpe sample variance:

$$
\hat{\text{SR}} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left( \text{SR}, \frac{1 + \text{SR}^2/2}{T} \right) \cdot \sqrt{q},
$$

for T observations (in years × q observations per year).

For monthly returns over 5 years (T = 60): standard error of Sharpe ≈ 0.13. A reported Sharpe of 1.0 has 90% CI ≈ [0.79, 1.21]. Not 0.95-1.05 as some intuit.

### Newey-West Correction

If returns are autocorrelated (most strategies have some), the effective sample size is smaller. Newey-West correction:

$$
\text{SE}(\hat{\text{SR}}) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \text{SR}^2/2}{T_{\text{eff}}}},
$$

where $T_{\text{eff}} = T / (1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^L (1 - k/(L+1)) \rho_k)$ adjusts for L-lag autocorrelation.

Typical strategies have $T_{\text{eff}} = 0.6 T$, widening CIs by ~30%.

### Reality Check — Sharpe Confidence

A "Sharpe of 2" from a 1-year backtest is essentially noise. Even from a 5-year backtest, the 90% CI is typically wide enough to include 1. Production decisions should account for this uncertainty.

## Deflated Sharpe Ratio

Bailey and López de Prado (2014) proposed the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)**:

$$
\text{DSR} = \frac{(\hat{\text{SR}} - \text{SR}_0) \sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3 \hat{\text{SR}} + (\gamma_4 - 1)/4 \hat{\text{SR}}^2}},
$$

where:
- $\hat{\text{SR}}$ is the observed Sharpe.
- $T$ is the number of observations.
- $\gamma_3$ is sample skewness; $\gamma_4$ is sample kurtosis.
- $\text{SR}_0 = \sqrt{\text{Var}_{\text{SR}}^{(N)}} (1 - \gamma_E + \gamma_E \Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/N))$, where N is the number of strategies tested and $\gamma_E$ is Euler's constant.

The $\text{SR}_0$ correction term accounts for multiple testing (selection of best of N). It is *not* an SE correction; it is the *expected maximum* Sharpe under the null.

### Interpretation

If DSR > 1.96, the strategy's Sharpe is significantly above the multiple-testing-adjusted benchmark, with α = 0.05.

### Implementation

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def deflated_sharpe(returns, num_trials=1, sr_0_method='dsr'):
    """Compute Deflated Sharpe Ratio."""
    T = len(returns)
    sr = returns.mean() / returns.std() * np.sqrt(252)  # annualized
    skew = ((returns - returns.mean())**3).mean() / returns.std()**3
    kurt = ((returns - returns.mean())**4).mean() / returns.std()**4
    
    if sr_0_method == 'dsr':
        # Expected maximum Sharpe under null with N trials
        gamma_E = 0.5772
        var_sr = (1 - skew*sr + (kurt - 1)/4 * sr**2) / (T - 1)
        sr_0 = np.sqrt(var_sr) * ((1 - gamma_E)*norm.ppf(1 - 1/num_trials) + 
                                    gamma_E*norm.ppf(1 - 1/(num_trials*np.e)))
    else:
        sr_0 = 0
    
    z = (sr - sr_0) * np.sqrt(T - 1) / np.sqrt(1 - skew*sr + (kurt - 1)/4 * sr**2)
    return z, sr_0

# Example: 1.5 Sharpe over 5 years, 1 trial
np.random.seed(0)
returns = np.random.normal(0.0006, 0.01, 1260)  # 5 years daily
z, sr_0 = deflated_sharpe(returns, num_trials=1)
print(f"DSR with 1 trial: z={z:.3f}, p-value={1 - norm.cdf(z):.4f}, SR_0={sr_0:.4f}")

# Same returns but if you tried 100 strategies
z, sr_0 = deflated_sharpe(returns, num_trials=100)
print(f"DSR with 100 trials: z={z:.3f}, p-value={1 - norm.cdf(z):.4f}, SR_0={sr_0:.4f}")
```

## Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio

The **Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR)** asks: what's the probability that the true Sharpe exceeds a threshold $\text{SR}^*$?

$$
\text{PSR}(\text{SR}^*) = \Phi\!\left( \frac{(\hat{\text{SR}} - \text{SR}^*) \sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \gamma_3 \hat{\text{SR}} + (\gamma_4 - 1)/4 \hat{\text{SR}}^2}} \right).
$$

For SR* = 0, PSR is the probability that the true Sharpe is positive. For SR* = 1, the probability that true Sharpe exceeds 1.

### Use

Set a threshold (e.g., 1.0) and accept strategies with PSR > 0.95. This is more interpretable than significance tests.

```python
def psr(returns, sr_star=1.0):
    sr = returns.mean() / returns.std() * np.sqrt(252)
    skew = ((returns - returns.mean())**3).mean() / returns.std()**3
    kurt = ((returns - returns.mean())**4).mean() / returns.std()**4
    T = len(returns)
    z = (sr - sr_star)*np.sqrt(T - 1) / np.sqrt(1 - skew*sr + (kurt - 1)/4 * sr**2)
    return norm.cdf(z)

print(f"PSR(SR > 1): {psr(returns, 1.0):.4f}")
```

## Probability of Backtest Overfitting

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, Zhu (2014). Compute the **PBO**:

1. Split the historical data into 2S non-overlapping subsets.
2. Combine the subsets into S "training" sets and S "test" sets. Each combination is a different way to split.
3. For each combination, find the best strategy in training, evaluate on test.
4. PBO = fraction of combinations where the best-in-training has below-median test performance.

Intuition: if strategies generalize, the best in-sample is also the best out-of-sample. If they're overfit, the best in-sample is randomly placed in OOS — half the time below median.

### Implementation

```python
import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

def pbo(strategies_returns, n_splits=16):
    """Compute PBO via combinatorial cross-validation."""
    M = strategies_returns.shape[0]  # observations
    N = strategies_returns.shape[1]  # strategies
    S = n_splits
    chunk_size = M // S
    
    # Split observations into S chunks
    chunks = [strategies_returns[i*chunk_size:(i+1)*chunk_size] for i in range(S)]
    
    overfit_count = 0
    n_combinations = 0
    
    # All combinations of S/2 chunks for training
    for train_idx in combinations(range(S), S // 2):
        n_combinations += 1
        train_data = np.vstack([chunks[i] for i in train_idx])
        test_data = np.vstack([chunks[i] for i in range(S) if i not in train_idx])
        
        # Best strategy in training (highest Sharpe)
        train_sr = train_data.mean(axis=0) / train_data.std(axis=0)
        best_strat = np.argmax(train_sr)
        
        # Test performance of that strategy
        test_sr = test_data.mean(axis=0) / test_data.std(axis=0)
        rank = np.sum(test_sr < test_sr[best_strat])
        if rank < N / 2:
            overfit_count += 1
    
    return overfit_count / n_combinations

# Example: 100 random strategies (no real edge)
np.random.seed(0)
M, N = 1000, 100
strategies = np.random.normal(0, 0.01, (M, N))
print(f"PBO for random strategies: {pbo(strategies):.3f}  (should be ~0.5)")
```

For real strategies, PBO < 0.2 indicates good out-of-sample behavior; > 0.5 is overfitting.

## Walk-Forward Analysis

Standard out-of-sample validation:
1. Split data into rolling train/test windows.
2. Optimize strategy parameters in train.
3. Evaluate on subsequent test (out-of-sample).
4. Roll forward.

### Anchored vs Rolling

- **Anchored**: training set grows over time (e.g., always uses 2015–N).
- **Rolling**: training set is fixed length (e.g., always uses last 5 years).

Anchored uses more data; rolling adapts faster to regime changes.

### Implementation

```python
def walk_forward(returns, train_size=252, test_size=63, n_steps=10, optimize_fn=None):
    """Walk-forward test."""
    n = len(returns)
    results = []
    for step in range(n_steps):
        train_start = step * test_size
        train_end = train_start + train_size
        test_end = train_end + test_size
        if test_end > n: break
        
        train_data = returns[train_start:train_end]
        test_data = returns[train_end:test_end]
        
        # Optimize on train
        params = optimize_fn(train_data) if optimize_fn else None
        
        # Evaluate on test
        test_sr = test_data.mean() / test_data.std() * np.sqrt(252)
        results.append({
            'train_start': train_start,
            'test_sr': test_sr,
            'params': params
        })
    return results
```

### Reality Check — Walk-Forward Limitations

Walk-forward is good but not perfect:
- Each test period is small; high variance.
- Parameter changes can be opportunistic.
- Doesn't address multiple-testing of strategies.

## Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation

López de Prado (2018) proposed CPCV for time-series data with limited data.

### Procedure

1. Split data into N folds.
2. For each combination of K test folds (out of N), train on the remaining N-K.
3. **Purge**: remove training samples that overlap with test samples (in time-windowed feature space).
4. **Embargo**: leave a buffer between train and test.
5. Aggregate test performance.

Total combinations: $\binom{N}{K}$, much larger than walk-forward N.

### Why Purge

If features are computed on overlapping windows (e.g., 20-day moving average), training on dates t-19 through t+19 has features that overlap with test on date t. This is implicit look-ahead. Purging removes the overlap.

### Embargo

Beyond purging the immediate overlap, embargo a buffer (e.g., 5-10 days) between train and test. This handles slowly-decaying signals.

```python
def purged_kfold_split(n_samples, n_folds, embargo_pct=0.01):
    """Generate purged k-fold splits."""
    fold_size = n_samples // n_folds
    embargo = int(n_samples * embargo_pct)
    splits = []
    for k in range(n_folds):
        test_start = k * fold_size
        test_end = test_start + fold_size
        # Train: all except test ± embargo
        train_idx = np.concatenate([
            np.arange(0, max(0, test_start - embargo)),
            np.arange(min(n_samples, test_end + embargo), n_samples)
        ])
        test_idx = np.arange(test_start, test_end)
        splits.append((train_idx, test_idx))
    return splits
```

## Time-Series Cross-Validation

Standard variants:

- **Expanding window**: train [0, t-1], test [t, t+T]. Train always grows.
- **Sliding window**: train [t-W, t-1], test [t, t+T]. Train fixed.
- **Blocked CV**: divide into N blocks; rotate which is test.

For time-series, never use random k-fold (which mixes time periods).

## Bootstrap and Resampling

For inference about a statistic without parametric assumptions.

### Block Bootstrap

For autocorrelated data:
1. Divide data into overlapping blocks of length L.
2. Sample blocks with replacement.
3. Compute statistic on resampled data.
4. Repeat many times.

Choice of L: rule of thumb L ≈ T^{1/3}. For T = 1000, L ≈ 10.

### Stationary Bootstrap

Politis-Romano (1994). Random block lengths from geometric distribution. Less sensitive to L choice.

### Moving Block Bootstrap

Kunsch (1989). Fixed-length blocks; resample with replacement.

```python
def block_bootstrap(returns, n_resamples=1000, block_size=10):
    """Block bootstrap of statistic on time series."""
    n = len(returns)
    stats = []
    for _ in range(n_resamples):
        # Sample blocks
        n_blocks = n // block_size + 1
        starts = np.random.randint(0, n - block_size, size=n_blocks)
        sample = np.concatenate([returns[s:s+block_size] for s in starts])[:n]
        stats.append(sample.mean() / sample.std() * np.sqrt(252))
    return np.array(stats)

bootstrap_sharpes = block_bootstrap(returns, 1000, 10)
print(f"Bootstrap Sharpe 90% CI: [{np.percentile(bootstrap_sharpes, 5):.3f}, {np.percentile(bootstrap_sharpes, 95):.3f}]")
```

## White's Reality Check

White (2000): test the best of N strategies for true alpha.

### Procedure

1. Compute Sharpe of each strategy.
2. Find best in-sample.
3. Bootstrap-resample data.
4. For each resample, recompute best Sharpe.
5. p-value = fraction of resamples with best Sharpe > observed best.

### Implementation Outline

```python
def whites_reality_check(strategies_returns, n_resamples=1000):
    """White's Reality Check."""
    obs_best_sr = np.max(strategies_returns.mean(axis=0) / strategies_returns.std(axis=0))
    bootstrap_best = []
    n_obs = strategies_returns.shape[0]
    for _ in range(n_resamples):
        idx = np.random.choice(n_obs, n_obs, replace=True)
        sample = strategies_returns[idx]
        sr_sample = sample.mean(axis=0) / sample.std(axis=0)
        bootstrap_best.append(sr_sample.max())
    p_value = np.mean(np.array(bootstrap_best) >= obs_best_sr)
    return p_value
```

If p < 0.05, the best strategy is significant after multiple-testing correction.

## Hansen's SPA Test

Hansen (2005): improvement on White's. Adjusts for strategies that are clearly worse than the benchmark, reducing power loss from including them.

The test uses a "studentized" max statistic:

$$
T^{\text{SPA}} = \max_k \frac{\sqrt{T}(\hat \mu_k - \hat \mu_0) - \mu_k}{\hat \sigma_k},
$$

with $\mu_k$ recentered toward zero for very-bad strategies.

Implementation: similar bootstrap structure to White's. See Hansen's original paper or `arch` Python package.

## Romano-Wolf Stepwise Testing

Romano-Wolf (2005): stepwise rejection of strategies, controlling family-wise error rate.

### Procedure

1. Compute test statistic for each strategy.
2. Bootstrap to get joint distribution.
3. Reject strategies in order, recursively.

### Use

For *which strategies* (not just any) are significant. Useful for identifying multiple winners.

## Transaction Cost Modeling

Real costs eat alpha. Realistic modeling:

### Spread Cost

Half-spread × volume traded. For SPX, ~0.5-1bp per trade.

### Market Impact

Square-root: $\sigma \sqrt{Q/V} \times Y$. For 1% ADV, ~5-15bp.

### Financing

Long positions financed; short positions earn rebate (if available).
- Long financing: ~5% APY (cash equivalent).
- Short rebate: 4% to negative for hard-to-borrow.

### Slippage

Difference between expected and realized fill price. Typically 1-5bp on top of spread.

### Total Cost Estimate

Pre-trade IS estimate (document 208):

$$
\text{Cost} = \tau \sigma + Y \sigma \sqrt{Q/V} + \text{spread} + \text{slippage}.
$$

For typical 1% ADV trade: 5-20bp depending on conditions.

### Reality Check — Cost Underestimation

Common pitfalls:
- Use "average" spread; tail conditions have wider.
- Assume zero slippage for backtests at close.
- Ignore opportunity cost of unfilled portion.

Production should use *worst-case* costs to be safe.

## Capacity Analysis

How big can the strategy get before alpha decays?

### Square-Root Capacity

For square-root impact, capacity is bounded by:

$$
\text{Capacity} \approx \frac{(\text{alpha bp})^2}{(\text{Y σ})^2 / V}.
$$

For an alpha of 30bp on σ = 1%, V = 1M shares/day, Y = 0.5: capacity ≈ 12% of ADV daily.

### Empirical Estimation

Run backtest at different sizes:
- 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 5%, 10% of ADV.
- Plot Sharpe vs size.
- Identify breakpoint where Sharpe drops below threshold.

### Reality Check — Capacity Reality

- Strategy alpha decays with size faster than backtest predicts.
- Capacity for live trading is typically 25-50% of "theoretical capacity."
- Once at capacity, growth is harder than theory.

## Regime Sensitivity

Test in different regimes:
- Bull / bear / sideways.
- Low / high vol.
- Pre / during / post crisis.
- Different decades.

A strategy that works in 2015-2019 but fails in 2020 is not a robust strategy.

### Stress Testing

Apply historical stress periods:
- 1987 crash.
- 1998 LTCM.
- 2000 dotcom.
- 2008 financial crisis.
- 2020 COVID.
- 2022 LDI / inflation.

Strategies that lose 30%+ in any of these may not survive a future similar event.

## Backtester Architecture

### Event-Driven vs Vectorized

- **Event-driven**: process events one at a time, simulating real-time. Slower but realistic.
- **Vectorized**: compute returns matrix-style. Fast but less realistic.

For HFT-like backtests: event-driven mandatory. For monthly factor backtests: vectorized adequate.

### Realistic Simulation

- Simulate order book dynamics.
- Track queue position.
- Account for partial fills.
- Model cancellation timing.

```python
class Backtester:
    def __init__(self, strategy, data):
        self.strategy = strategy
        self.data = data
        self.positions = {}
        self.cash = 1_000_000
    
    def run(self):
        for tick in self.data:
            # Strategy generates orders
            orders = self.strategy.on_tick(tick)
            # Simulate fills with realistic costs
            for order in orders:
                fill = self.simulate_fill(order, tick)
                if fill:
                    self.update_position(fill)
        return self.compute_pnl()
```

## Realistic Execution Simulation

### Queue Position

When you submit a limit order:
1. Estimate your queue position based on current depth.
2. As the queue ahead is consumed, your position improves.
3. As new orders join behind you, your position stays.
4. As limit orders are cancelled ahead of you, your position improves.

Sophisticated simulators track this; naive assume immediate fill.

### Partial Fills

Large orders often fill partially. Simulation must:
- Track filled vs unfilled.
- Handle partial fills realistically.
- Account for the unfilled portion's exposure to market moves.

### Cancellation Dynamics

Real markets have high cancel rates (>95% of submitted orders cancel). Simulator must:
- Model how often orders age out.
- Model cross-side aggressive flow.
- Model book replenishment.

## VWAP/TWAP Simulation

For benchmark-aware execution:
- Pre-compute volume profile.
- Simulate slice-by-slice execution.
- Apply realistic spread + impact at each slice.
- Compare to benchmark price.

Results inform choice of execution algorithm.

## Borrow Cost and Short Locate

For short-side strategies:
- **Borrow rate**: cost of borrowing the security to short.
- **Locate availability**: not all stocks are easily borrowable.

Hard-to-borrow stocks:
- Often the same names as small/illiquid.
- Borrow rates can be 10-50% APY.
- Locate may be unavailable, preventing the short.

Backtest must:
- Model borrow rates from a reliable source.
- Mark stocks as unborrowable (e.g., use Wolverine or Markit hard-to-borrow lists).
- Skip shorts on unborrowable stocks.

## Funding and Margin

### Margin Requirements

- **Reg T**: 50% initial margin for stocks.
- **Portfolio margin**: lower, based on net risk.
- **Crypto**: varies by exchange and venue.

Strategies levered above margin are infeasible.

### Settlement

- T+0 (crypto): immediate.
- T+1 (US equities, post-2024): one business day.
- T+2 (older equity, FX spot): two business days.

For backtests: assume T+0 funds available in most cases; T+1+ for capital-flow-sensitive strategies.

## Tax Considerations

For taxable accounts:
- **Wash sales**: cannot harvest tax loss if same security bought within 30 days. Backtests for taxable accounts should respect this.
- **Holding period**: long-term gains (1 year+) taxed at lower rate. May incentivize patience.
- **Short-term vs long-term**: different rates.

For institutional / tax-advantaged: ignore taxes. For retail: tax-aware backtests can show different optimal strategies.

## Backtest to Live Divergence

When live trading underperforms backtest:

### Diagnostic Steps

1. **Audit data**: ensure live data matches backtest data.
2. **Audit costs**: are live costs higher than modeled?
3. **Audit signal**: is the live signal computed identically?
4. **Audit execution**: are fills realistic?
5. **Audit regime**: has the market regime changed?
6. **Audit competition**: is alpha decay from crowding?

### Common Causes

- **Overfitting**: strategy was tuned on the test set.
- **Selection bias**: only winners published.
- **Cost underestimation**: real costs > modeled.
- **Regime change**: signal stopped working.
- **Latency**: backtest assumed instant execution.

### Reality Check — Live-Backtest Gap

Even good strategies typically deliver 50-70% of backtest Sharpe in live. Plan for this.

---

## Paper Trading and Capital Ramp

Before going live:
1. Paper trade for 1-3 months.
2. Compare paper to backtest.
3. If paper matches, allocate small capital.
4. Monitor for 1-2 quarters.
5. Ramp gradually if performance holds.

### Reality Check — Paper Trading Limitations

Paper trading misses:
- Market impact (your orders don't actually trade).
- Adverse selection on fills.
- Operational issues at scale.

Paper trading is a sanity check, not a guarantee.

---

## Strategy Decay Monitoring

After deployment:
- Track rolling performance.
- Compare to backtest expectations.
- Detect early signs of decay.

### Decay Signals

- Sharpe drops below 50% of backtest for 3+ months.
- Maximum drawdown exceeds backtest worst-case.
- Realized Sharpe in any regime substantially differs from backtest.

### When to Kill

Decision tree:
- Performance below threshold for 6 months → reduce capital.
- Performance below threshold for 12 months → kill.
- Drawdown exceeds 1.5× backtest max → reassess.

Document 219 covers the psychology of strategy retirement.

---

## Reality Checks

- **Statistical rigor isn't optional**: most "alpha" doesn't survive proper testing.
- **Multiple testing is everywhere**: be honest about how many strategies you've tried.
- **Costs matter more than signal in many cases**: optimize execution before optimizing alpha.
- **Live is harder than backtest**: plan for 50-70% performance in live.
- **Strategy decay happens**: be ready to kill or adapt.
